The University of Sydney’s graduate-entry MD programme attracts me precisely because of what it assumes about its students: that a completed undergraduate degree brings intellectual maturity, disciplinary breadth, and a clearer sense of purpose that enriches the study of medicine itself. The diversity of backgrounds within any Sydney MD cohort, with students arriving from science, humanities, law, and engineering among others, creates a richer learning environment than a programme drawing exclusively from school leavers. The university’s research standing and its affiliated hospitals, including Royal Prince Alfred and Westmead, offer access to exceptional clinical complexity across a genuinely diverse metropolitan population. I am also drawn to Sydney’s serious engagement with Indigenous health and the embedding of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspectives throughout the curriculum rather than treating them as supplementary content. The city’s scale and diversity mean that developing as a culturally competent clinician is not an aspiration but a daily practice, and I find that demanding and important in equal measure.

Group Project Examples:
Working in a group project for SSI (an NGO) in university that was a term long and a person was, on paper, part of our group but never showed up even once.

In my 3rd year, I was part of a four-person group working with SSI, an NGO, on a term-long project, A proposal.

One group member was enrolled and listed on the roster but never attended a single meeting, in person or online, across the entire term.

I noticed this when looking over the registration sheets we had to fill in at the start. I asked the other group members how best to respond to this, so we could gain a communal opinion. 

I said I would try to reach out to them. Reached out through different channels, message, email. No response still. During this time I kept a record of my attempts to reach her. 

As a group we agreed to redistribute and contact to coordinator if we hadn't heard from the missing member by a certain cut off date. 

When that date passed with no contact, I emailed the coordinator, explained what we'd tried, and asked for guidance, being careful to frame it factually rather than as a complaint.

We submitted on time and got a strong mark. The coordinator used our documentation to adjust individual marks through the peer assessment component.

It taught me the difference between quietly absorbing someone else's share — which feels like the "nice" thing to do but breeds resentment and doesn't scale, and addressing it as a team early, with a clear process.

And also raising issues early before they become bigger problems
