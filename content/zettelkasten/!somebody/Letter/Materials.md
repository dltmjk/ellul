At a random feast given by an aristocrat of Thessaly, the poet "Simonides of Ceos" chanted a lyrical song which honoured both his host, Scopas, and to two divine brothers, Castor and Pollux. The Gemini twins who sailed with Jason and his Argonauts. When Simonides asked payment for his panegyric, Scopas, either being miserly or mean, told the poet that he would pay only half of the agreed upon sum, then telling him to 'obtain the rest from those twin God's who he had previously praised.' 

Right then, a porter reported to Simonides that two young brothers were waiting outside who wished to see him immediately. He rose from his seat and exited the banquet-hall to find no-one and as he searched for those who called him, he heard a thundering sound. The roof of the banquet-hall had collapsed! crushing everybody inside. It seems that Castor and Pollux had paid their fair share of their debt by bringing 'Simon' away from his death. However, those other unlucky guests had no active debt and, as such, had been squashed to the state where their relatives could not certainly identify them. Despite their mangled bodies, Simonides was able to match corpse to relative by reference to the place he saw them sitting, learning in the process how important an orderly structure and place were for a proper memory.

Or In Cicero's words:

Thus the order of places would preserve the order of things, and the symbols of the things would denote the things themselves; so that we should use the places as waxen tablets, and the symbols as letters.

I've always felt this was a very odd story. 
It's told by Cicero in the beginning of his *De Oratore*, and by Quintilian in his *Institutio Oratoria*. More famously, in the 'Ad Herennium' thought to have been written by Cicero, but actually was penned by an unknown doctor for his friend 'Herennius'. It seems odd to start a letter, meant for a birthday, with a gruesome story told a thousand years ago about the cultivation of une bonne mémoire. I think my fixation with memory makes a little more sense considering our separation. 

On writing on the death of his wife, C.S. Lewis recounted an experience "meet a man I haven’t seen for ten years. And all that time I had thought I was remembering him well—how he looked and spoke and
the sort of things he said. The first five minutes of the real man shattered the image completely."

The most recent time I felt like this is when I saw that video of you talking, and it was your gestures that surprised me. It was like encountering a foreign word, learned long ago but since forgotten, with it's translation placed alongside it. "yes, ofcourse, how could it mean anything else but that?." And, similarly, how could you gesture in any other possible way? It's you after all. 

Lewis had forgotten such little things, like the way the man jerked his head, or slammed his drink, or maybe the way he held his stomach before letting loose gas. One is definitely bound to forget many things, like how somebody would keep their phone in their back pocket, or how one could fill up on soup and have no space for anything else. Or the maybe way one slept on their side hugging a pillow: "I had known all these things once and I recognised them the moment I met them again." (2)

He knew that the same thing would happen to the memory of his wife "Slowly, quietly, like snow-flakes—like the small flakes that come when it is going to snow all night—little flakes of me, my impressions, my selections, are settling down on the image of her. The real shape will be quite hidden in the end. Ten minutes—ten seconds—of the real H. would correct all this."

We're far luckier than Lewis because none of us are dead. We're bound for correction, over and over again, running into the others impressions, selections, images, and kisses. The moment I see you after I come back will correct a hundred things (not that I'm prone to forgetting, but that's just how much strikes me about you), all "engraved with the sharpness of steel." (3) 

===Not exactly steel, because what comes after correction is the sculpting softly. That is, continuously changing for each other, from the others hands.===

Still I didn't want to forget. The point of Simonides' story was to show that one could remember nearly anything if it was put into a structured, spatial form. That is, into a location. The method: create a set of images which one would encounter, in a designated order, while moving through a familiar. I had my doubts, maybe the "method of loci" with "all these places and images would only bury under a heap of rubble whatever little one does remember naturally." (4)

Still, Joyce was only able to write about Dublin when he was in Paris, in his 'exile.' I find Sydney is far nicer when I'm away from it. Always easier to forget about the parts you dislike, King Street is a straight road made up of Master Rice Lyn; Elizabeth's; Uncanny, and Reunion. It's a different thing with people. But you're so important to me you could make anything into anything else, and you could certainly make me try this.
