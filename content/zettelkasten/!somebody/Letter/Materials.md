At a random feast given by an aristocrat of Thessaly, the poet "Simonides of Ceos" chanted a lyrical song which honoured both his host, Scopas, and two divine brothers: Castor and Pollux, the Gemini twins who sailed with Jason and his Argonauts. After his performance, Simonides asked payment for his panegyric and Scopas, either being miserly or mean, told the poet that he would pay only half of the agreed upon sum. Telling him to 'obtain the rest from those twin God's who he had previously praised.' 

Right then, a porter reported to Simonides that two young brothers were waiting outside who wished to see him. He rose from his seat and exited the banquet-hall to find no-one and as he searched for those who called him, he heard a thundering sound. The roof of the banquet-hall had collapsed! crushing everybody inside. It seems that Castor and Pollux had paid their fair share of their debt by bringing 'Simon' away from his death. However, those other unlucky guests had no active debt and, as such, had been squashed to a state where their relatives could not hope to identify them. Despite their mangled bodies, Simonides was able to match corpse to person by reference to the place he saw them sitting, learning in the process how important an orderly structure and place were for a proper memory.

Or In Cicero's words:

"Thus the order of places would preserve the order of things, and the symbols of the things would denote the things themselves; so that we should use the places as waxen tablets, and the symbols as letters."

Do Latin with translation underneath. 

I've always felt this was a very odd story. 
It's told by Cicero in the beginning of his *De Oratore*, and by Quintilian in his *Institutio Oratoria*. More famously, in the 'Ad Herennium' (thought to have been written by Cicero, but actually was penned by an unknown doctor for his friend 'Herennius') It seems odd to start a letter, a birthday letter at that, with a gruesome story told two thousand years ago about the cultivation of une bonne mémoire. I think my fixation with memory makes a little more sense considering our separation, 3 months as of my writing. 

On attempting to process on the death of his wife, C.S. Lewis told of when he met a man he hadn't "seen for ten years. And all that time I had thought I was remembering him well—how he looked and spoke and the sort of things he said. The first five minutes of the real man shattered the image completely."

The most recent time I felt like this is when I saw that video of you talking, and it was your gestures that surprised me. It was like encountering a foreign word, learned long ago but since forgotten, and the translation of the word placed adjacent. "yes, ofcourse, how could it mean anything else but that?." And, similarly, how could you gesture in any other possible way? It's you after all.

Lewis had forgotten such little things, like the way the man jerked his head, or slammed his drink, or maybe the way he held his stomach before letting loose gas. Yes, one is definitely bound to forget many things, like how somebody would keep their phone in their back pocket, or how one could fill up on soup and have no space for anything else. Or the maybe way one slept on their side hugging a pillow: "I had known all these things once and I recognised them the moment I met them again." (2)

Worst of all, he knew that the same process was at work in his memory of his wife "Slowly, quietly, like snow-flakes—like the small flakes that come when it is going to snow all night—little flakes of me, my impressions, my selections, are settling down on the image of her. The real shape will be quite hidden in the end. Ten minutes—ten seconds—of the real H. would correct all this."

We're far luckier than Lewis because none of us are dead. We're bound for correction, over and over again, running into the others impressions, selections, images, and kisses. The moment I see you after I come back will correct a hundred things (not that I'm prone to forgetting, but that's just how much strikes me about you), all "engraved with the sharpness of steel." (3) 

===Not exactly steel, because what comes after correction is the sculpting softly. That is, continuously changing for each other, from the others hands.===

Still I didn't want to forget. The point of Simonides' story was to show that one could remember nearly anything if it was put into a structured, spatial form. That is, into a location. The method: create a set of images which one would encounter, in a designated order, while moving through a familiar. I had my doubts, maybe the "method of loci" (or the "memory palace") with "all these places and images would only bury under a heap of rubble whatever little one does remember naturally." (4) 

Still, Joyce was only able to write about Dublin when he was in Paris, in his 'exile.' I find Sydney is far nicer when I'm away from it. Always easier to forget about the parts you dislike, King Street is a straight road made up of Master Rice Lyn; Elizabeth's; Uncanny, and Reunion. It's a different thing with people and while I could easily swallow forgetting Lachlan (both), and maybe I could stomach forgetting David (sorry David) I cannot even entertain the idea of forgetting you. 

So maybe I'll try placing together those disjoint memories in an orderly fashion, to avoid the snow.

Now obviously I can't place *every* memory I have of you in order, it wouldn't be possible. First, there are too many memories, so many in-fact that there wouldn't be enough paper in the world! Don't believe me? I can prove it:

Let's say that a memory is a set made up of multiple moments or a single moment. A moment is, for our purposes, defined as a continuous (not instantaneous) stretch of time in which one major action or a few minor actions are carried out (1). I think each moment would require, at the very least, one A4 sized page of font-sized 12 writing. That's so small, and too much writing! (I can hear you say), I actually think it's an underestimate. Take for example your eyes, describing that exact gradient of blue would take at least a thousand words, then we look up and have the problem of your hair (another thousand, complete with full instructions for proper washing), and each of your moles (each at least a hundred), and then your teeth which would require a word-count of at least a BILLI- ... - Do you see how easily this gets out of hand? But fine, one A4 paper for the sake of neatness. 

We met on August 20th, 2 years ago. That first book club meeting ran for two hours, following meetings ran for a similar time. One every two weeks, an eternity by contemporary standards. Could you imagine texting once every two weeks? Easier for a double suicide frankly. But we press on! By 2025 Jan - I calculate we've spent a total of 8 book club sessions together, or 16 hours, if each hour contains 120 moments (each moment being 30 seconds average(3)) then at that point we had enough memories to fill 2000 pages. 

Then we start falling in love. The bad thing about memory is that it isn't picky enough when somebody you love is involved. It soaks up everything so you end up remembering them but also every stupid unimportant thing around them. This is why I remember the music they played at that birthday rave, why I can sketch each of those amateur exhibits that gallery. Because the context is exceptionally important. That moment I saw you on the hill was easily a multi-volume series of books. 

But let's say: an average of 3 hangouts each week, minus four months of me being overseas (for which the due reparations will be paid!), properly accounting for the overflow memories from vacations in Melbourne and Berry, and we have ... nearly 200,000 pages of us (4). That is 100 million words, 20 meters of paper, and five thousand five hundred and fifty five hours of reading(5). 

Not to brag, but I've actually read all of it by virtue of living it and loving you.
