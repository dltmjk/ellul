On the 18th of December, in a room or maybe an office. Maybe a country east of somewhere and perhaps landlocked. An adolescent boy, now aged thirty, and at the time aged only 18 (standing completely still) thought about his life as he would proceed to live it. And the later man of 30, sitting with his hands slightly fidgeting, thought about that boy of eighteen who he grew out of but could no longer understand. Both of them share the same fears inherited from the child cheerfully collecting snails, cherries, slugs, fears, and neuroticisms.

The man of 30 is surrounded ==🟣by a landscape or maybe by=== concrete. He is drinking something cold and he has refused, for the past hour or so, to look outside his window. Even now, he hasn't realised exactly what had happened to him. He knew that it had occurred, but he knew it like a fact. Which is to say he didn't know it at all. He knew it the same way he knew andromeda or the atom, or that the yellow sun in the sky was actually a white ball of fire. This latter fact made complete sense to him despite never having seen a white flame in his life. He could say all this because other people had told him, the same way they'd been telling him about John.  

To give him a little credit, He must've known a little, otherwise he wouldn't have been running around like he did, calling people, scheduling cars and flights. What he still didn't know, prevented the one thing necessary thing and perhaps the only thing John would've actually wanted. He was like a child hearing his parents argue, understanding their anger but not what their words mean. Perhaps this explained why was able to handle it with such ease. Dealing tactfully when people would walk up to him to apologize, for his tremendous loss. The tact was in not correcting them. If there was a loss he needed to be shown it. 

The boy of 18 is staring straight through the window placed above his desk. He has not started fidgeting with his hands yet. Yesterday he had learned how to play the Turkish March, John had taught it to him while they bounced from music store to railway piano. The young boy didn't own a piano and John's mother wouldn't let him inside the house. ==🟢He practiced on rattling keys, an underground piano kept in a tunnel below railway tracks. ==

He didn't know how to tune it, so his playing never sounded right. It barely mattered, he enjoyed the weight of the keys, holding up sheet music, pretending to be able to read it. Most of all pretending to be someone (a musician didn't have any fears or his worries, least of all his fears and worries. They all wore nicely ironed black socks). Every note reminded of him John. The first time they met he lied and told John he could play but John knew he was lying and smiled. Before heading home, he would slam the piano shut, which is what he thought a musician would do. As he walked out the tunnel he felt, directly above him, that the sun had started it's shining again. The light meant people could see and not only hear him, leaving meant putting on his old wet socks again. 

Everyday he woke up and had the fears he had yesterday with the worries he had yesterday and, even as a child, had wished to wake up with somebody else's fears, their worries, their life. 

 The first time we met I lied and told him I could play but he saw right through me. It still seems to me that I could never get anything past him. 

He taught me how to play the Turkish March, at least the first page, and he taught it to me in music stores. Because I didn't own a piano and his mother would never let me inside the house. I practiced on an out of tune piano kept in the underground in a tunnel that nobody ever crossed.

Maybe it was because he knew that nothing was as terrifying as he originally imagined it to be. No failure had stung or suffocated him like he assumed they would. Humiliation never came exactly like how he had thought it would, it never took the shape he supposed it to be. After all, everything still had to be lived through, so death (being nothing new to life) would likely be the same. In fact, it would proably feel like slipping into a familiar bed with familiar sheets after a long day. Freshly washed and dried clothes that you haven't worn since you were a child. 

Or maybe it was because he could still imagine John, in-between this pointless exercise of mourning him,  waiting at home. Wondering where everybody was. 


He was reminded of that time he was alone in his parents house, clearing their belongings after they both passed. It was warm, or at least it felt warm, or maybe it was because he strongly remembered the sun, and the sunlight as instantly filled the now empty room. Death also seemed similar to this. Though he couldn't tell you exactly why. John was full of these associations, and it was recently that he started to realize the amount. He was like a unifying principle so large and absolutely simple that anything and everything could be traced back to him, his fingerprints smudged everywhere.
