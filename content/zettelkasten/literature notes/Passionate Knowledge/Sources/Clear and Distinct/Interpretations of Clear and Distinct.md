There are many competing interpretations of C&D truth. I've found two that align with yours (i.e., they argue that C&D knowledge does not arrive at actual truth and is a purely logical procedure which depends on God): 

-

For interpretations of what clear and distinct knowledge requires; see Frankfurt (1970) and Gewirth (1941, 1970)

___
Frankfurt, Harry. Demons, Dreamers, and Madmen. Indianapolis: Bobbs-Merrill, 1970.

Frankfurt argues that C&D perception is just a logical procedure to ensure that no grounds for doubt can exist. He uses the following passage as proof: 

"What is it to us if someone should perhaps imagine that the very thing of whose truth we have
been so firmly persuaded appears false to God or to an angel and that as a consequence it is false
speaking absolutely? What do we care about this absolute falsity, since we by no means believe in
it or even have the least suspicion of it? For we are supposing a persuasion so firm, that it can in
no way be removed–a persuasion, therefore, that is exactly the same as the most perfect certainty.
(AT 7:145)"

To argue that “Descartes evidently recognises that his position entails that from our knowing something with perfect certitude it does not follow that it is, ‘speaking absolutely’ true” (pg. 179). Therefore, C&D Knowledge is unreliable. 

Arguments Against Frankfurt 
As a brief aside, arguing against this, and similar, "subjective" interpretations has become a bit of a cottage industry. Out of what I've seen, I think this remark by Margaret Wilson, is the most interesting: “Frankfurt imputes to Descartes a distinction between concern with certainty and concern with truth that is not really borne out by the texts” (236 n42). Not worth citing, but interesting to think about. 

Wilson, Margaret. Descartes. London: Routledge, 1991.

Alan Gewirth, “The Cartesian Circle” and “The Cartesian Circle Reconsidered”.

Gewirth, in both 1941 and 1970 (However, I've only cited his 1970 paper here. His 1941 paper is Gewirth, Alan. The Cartesian Circle, 1941), makes a similar point to Frankfurt that C&D is not concerned with truth but with coherence or the logical consistency of reasoning. For him, certainty requires knowledge that our ideas *actually* correspond to objects outside of our mind. Therefore, C&D as simple characteristics of ideas cannot guarantee this and are *fundamentally* the same as sense perception and only with knowledge of God can C&D perception become legitimate. 

Gewirth, Alan. “The Cartesian Circle.” The Philosophical Review 50:4 (July, 1941):
368-395. (Author’s name published as “Gewirtz”)

Gewirth, Alan. “The Cartesian Circle Reconsidered.” Journal of Philosophy 67 (1970): 668-685.
